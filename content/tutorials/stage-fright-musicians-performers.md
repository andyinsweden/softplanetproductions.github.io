*By John Montgomery*

The lights change. Someone gives you a nod. The opening bar is almost here—and suddenly the piece you knew yesterday feels a little further away.

If that sounds familiar, you have plenty of company. Performing can feel exciting and uncomfortable at the same time. You do not have to wait until every butterfly disappears before you share your music.

What helps is having something practical to do next: an opening you trust, a cue you recognise and a way to carry on if a note goes astray.

## Give yourself a friendly start

Prepare the first phrase with particular care. For a pianist, that might mean silently finding the opening hand position and hearing the tempo before beginning. For a singer or actor, it could mean knowing your entrance, first breath and first intention in the scene.

Rehearse that start several times, including the few seconds before it. Sit down, settle, look up and begin. Familiarity with the whole little sequence can make the opening feel less like a leap into the unknown.

This is preparation for the rest of the performance too; it does not replace learning the complete piece.

## Make the room part of your preparation

Arrive with enough time to find your place and check what you need. Adjust the bench, organise page turns, test the sound and identify where your cues will come from.

Small details deserve attention. Can you see the conductor? Is the music stand at a comfortable height? Does your opening patch have the right volume? A short check can remove a surprisingly distracting question from your mind.

If you are in a production, follow the stage manager’s and musical director’s instructions. Your routine should fit the show’s call times and backstage arrangements.

## Breathe comfortably and let yourself settle

You do not need an elaborate breathing challenge before going on. The NHS recommends gentle, regular breathing without forcing the breath, practising for a few minutes when possible. Try that during ordinary practice sessions so it becomes familiar.

Let your shoulders soften, notice the support of the floor or seat and give yourself time. Avoid forcing large breaths or long breath holds. If a breathing exercise makes you light-headed, stop and return to normal breathing.

For singers and wind players, keep your normal technique and any warm-up agreed with your teacher. A calming routine should support your playing, rather than become another task to get right.

## Put your attention into the music

Choose one helpful focus for the next phrase. Perhaps it is the shape of the melody, the bass pulse, your scene partner’s words or the conductor’s next cue.

“Let this phrase sing” is a more useful instruction at the keyboard than an ongoing review of every finger movement. In musical theatre, a clear character intention can give you something concrete to communicate.

You may still feel nervous. That is all right: the aim is to give your attention a useful home, not to demand a particular feeling.

## Practise carrying on

A missed note need not become a missed passage. During practice, occasionally play through a complete section without stopping to repair every slip. Afterwards, return to the places that need work.

Choose a few recovery landmarks: a rehearsal letter, a clear chord change, the beginning of the next phrase. In ensemble playing, keep listening and follow the conductor or agreed cues. If you lose your place, rejoin at a point you can identify confidently.

Try this with a friend or a recording device before an important performance. A short, low-pressure run-through can reveal what needs attention while giving you practice at finishing.

## A simple pre-show routine

1. **Check your setup.** Music, instrument, sound and sightlines are ready.
2. **Warm up gently.** Use the familiar routine that suits your instrument or voice.
3. **Settle your breathing.** Keep it comfortable and unforced.
4. **Recall the opening.** Hear the tempo and know your first cue.
5. **Choose your focus.** A phrase, a lyric, a scene partner or the conductor.

Afterwards, write down one thing that went well and one thing to practise next. Give yourself credit for showing up and making music. Confidence can grow through many manageable experiences, rather than one perfect night.

## Keep the enjoyment in view

There is room for both preparation and pleasure in a performance. You have a melody to share, a story to tell or a part to contribute. Start there, take the next cue and give yourself permission to enjoy a good moment when it arrives.
