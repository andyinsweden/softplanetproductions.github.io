*By John Montgomery*

The right piano makes you want to sit down and play. Perhaps that means a straightforward instrument for your first chords, a compact keyboard for rehearsals, or a more substantial action for exploring every shade of a favourite piece.

If you are comparing the **Yamaha P-45 vs P-225 vs P-525**, the biggest differences are **keyboard action, piano samples, polyphony and portability**. All three offer 88 weighted keys and built-in speakers, but they suit different players and budgets.

These are **portable digital pianos** that can also be used for performances. This is a comparison based on Yamaha’s published specifications, rather than a hands-on test of the three instruments.

## Which Yamaha digital piano should you choose?

**Quick answer:** The **Yamaha P-45** is a straightforward choice if you find one at an attractive price and mainly want to learn piano fundamentals. The **Yamaha P-225** is the strongest middle-ground option for many home pianists who want a compact instrument with a more developed sound engine. Choose the **Yamaha P-525** if its GrandTouch-S action, richer sound options and more advanced controls matter enough to justify the extra weight and expense.

These are **recommendations based on published specifications**, not a claim that one keyboard feels best to every player. The most expensive instrument is not automatically the best fit.

- **Best for a simple, budget-conscious setup:** Yamaha P-45 — particularly when a used or discounted example is good value.
- **Best balance of portability and features:** Yamaha P-225 — a practical step up without the P-525's carrying weight.
- **Best for touch-focused players:** Yamaha P-525 — worth trying in person if expressive action and sound variety take priority.

## Yamaha P-45 vs P-225 vs P-525 specifications compared

| Feature | P-45 | P-225 | P-525 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Keyboard action | Graded Hammer Standard (GHS) | Graded Hammer Compact (GHC) | GrandTouch-S; wooden white keys |
| Main piano sound | AWM stereo sampling | Yamaha CFX | Yamaha CFX and Bösendorfer Imperial |
| Maximum polyphony | 64 notes | 192 notes | 256 notes |
| Weight | 11.5 kg | 11.5 kg | 22 kg |
| Built-in voices | 10 | 24 | 44 voices, plus other instrument and drum sound sets |
| Keyboard keys | 88 weighted | 88 weighted | 88 weighted |
| Main attraction | Simplicity | Compact size with more musical options | More elaborate piano action and sound features |

Polyphony is the number of notes an instrument can sound simultaneously. Sustain and layered voices use that capacity too; the bigger numbers give more headroom, rather than telling you how many keys you can press!

### What is the difference between GHS, GHC and GrandTouch-S?

All three are weighted actions, but they are not interchangeable. **GHS** is Yamaha’s Graded Hammer Standard action on the P-45. **GHC** means Graded Hammer Compact, designed for the slimmer P-225. The P-525's **GrandTouch-S** includes wooden white keys and escapement. These specifications describe different designs; they cannot tell you which action your hands will prefer. Test soft dynamics, repeated notes and slower passages before choosing.

### Yamaha P-45 vs P-225 vs P-525: sound and headphones

The P-45 uses Yamaha's AWM stereo sampling, whereas the P-225 features the **Yamaha CFX** piano sound and VRM Lite. The P-525 includes **CFX and Bösendorfer Imperial** piano voices, with additional expression modelling. If you mostly play through headphones, compare the sounds that matter to you at a sensible listening volume rather than judging only from loudspeaker demos.

The P-225 pairs Yamaha’s CFX piano sound with the GHC action. It has 24 voices, split and layer functions, and VRM Lite resonance modelling. That gives you more scope to move between solo piano, a layered texture and a keyboard part for a rehearsal.

At 11.5 kg, it weighs the same as the P-45, while its cabinet is shallower. If your piano shares a room with everyday life—or travels to rehearsals—that is a practical benefit.

**Our take:** a promising middle option for players who want more flexibility in a compact instrument. Try the action yourself: its compact design does not tell you whether it will suit your hands better than another keyboard.

The P-525 offers GrandTouch-S action with wooden white keys, synthetic ebony and ivory key surfaces, and escapement. Its piano voices include Yamaha CFX and Bösendorfer Imperial, with additional resonance and expression modelling features.

For headphone practice, Yamaha specifies binaural sampling for the **CFX Grand voice**. That detail matters: it should not be read as a claim that every piano voice uses binaural sampling.

The practical trade-off is weight. At 22 kg before a case or stand, this is a different carrying proposition from the other two.

**Our take:** consider it if detailed piano playing is your priority and the feel justifies the extra cost and weight for you. No specification can promise that an action will feel exactly like your favourite acoustic grand.

## Is the Yamaha P-225 worth upgrading to from the P-45?

**Potentially, yes — if you want the P-225's CFX sound, higher 192-note polyphony, compact cabinet and broader voice selection.** The P-45's 64-note polyphony and simpler sound engine are not necessarily limitations for basic piano practice, so an upgrade just for a larger specification number may be unnecessary.

If you are unhappy with the **feel** of your P-45, try the GHC action on the P-225 before buying. It is a different design, not a universally better keyboard. If your priority is a markedly different action and richer piano features, audition the P-525 as well.

## Which Yamaha piano is best for beginners?

For someone starting piano lessons, all three have the key foundation: **88 weighted keys**. The P-45 can be an effective, economical practice instrument when priced appropriately. The P-225 offers more room to explore different sounds while remaining portable. The P-525 may make sense for a committed player with a larger budget, but its extra features are not required to learn scales, chords, reading or hand independence.

A comfortable bench, a stable stand and a suitable sustain pedal can make more difference to your daily practice than paying for features you rarely use. Check the package contents rather than assuming accessories are included.

## Which is better for gigging, moving or a small room?

At **11.5 kg each**, the P-45 and P-225 are much easier to move than the **22 kg P-525**. The P-225 also has a shallower main cabinet than the P-45. For rehearsals or small rooms, that may be more important than adding premium features. If the instrument stays in one place, the P-525's weight becomes less of an obstacle.

## Give each piano the same five-minute audition

Take a short piece you know well and use it on every instrument. Play a gentle phrase, repeat it more firmly, then try a few chords with the sustain pedal. Listen through speakers and your own headphones if possible.

Ask yourself:

- Can I control quiet notes comfortably?
- Does the touch encourage me to shape the phrase?
- Are the sounds and controls useful for the music I actually play?
- Can I manage the instrument, stand and case when travelling?

Check the full purchase cost too: a suitable stand, bench, pedal and transport protection can change the comparison. Prices and availability vary by country and retailer, so confirm the current offer and what is included.

## Frequently asked questions about Yamaha P-Series pianos

### Which is better, Yamaha P-45 or P-225?

The P-225 has more voices, higher polyphony and a different weighted action, making it more versatile on paper. The P-45 can still be good value for straightforward piano practice. Price and personal preference for the keyboard action should decide between them.

### Is the Yamaha P-525 worth the extra money?

It can be for pianists prioritising GrandTouch-S action, CFX and Bösendorfer piano voices and expanded performance features. It is substantially heavier than the P-45 and P-225. Try one in person and compare the total price with stand, pedal and any other accessories you need.

### Does the Yamaha P-225 have weighted keys?

Yes. It has **88 Graded Hammer Compact (GHC) keys**. Weighted action helps pianists practise control, but how an action feels remains a personal decision.

### Is the Yamaha P-45 still good for learning piano?

Yes. Its 88-key GHS weighted keyboard and simple controls can support lessons and daily technique practice, especially if it is priced competitively. Consider the P-225 or other current models when comparing new purchases.

### What is the main difference between the Yamaha P-225 and P-525?

The P-225 focuses on compact portability with GHC action and the CFX piano sound. The P-525 offers GrandTouch-S action with wooden white keys, more sound and expression features, and a heavier body.

### Can I connect these pianos to a computer?

All three have **USB-to-host** connectivity, useful for connecting to compatible music software. Check the required cables, drivers and software support for your own computer before purchase.
