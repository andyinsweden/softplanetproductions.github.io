Playing with both hands can seem difficult when you are still deciding which notes to play. The secret is to make one hand **predictable** while the other experiments. For this beginner piano improvisation exercise, your left hand will provide a simple pulse and your right hand will invent a short melody.

You only need a piano or keyboard. Begin slowly; a comfortable tempo matters more than speed.

## Step 1: Establish the left-hand foundation

Find a C below middle C. Count aloud **1, 2, 3, 4** and play that low C on **beat 1**. Let it ring while you count beats 2, 3 and 4. Repeat for four bars.

The left hand now has only one job: return to beat 1. If your rhythm is uneven, tap your foot or count aloud before you add another note.

## Step 2: Introduce the right hand

Find **C, D, E and G** around middle C. Keep playing the lower C on beat 1 and try this small right-hand phrase:

| Beat | Left hand | Right hand |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Low C | C |
| 2 | Rest | D |
| 3 | Rest | E |
| 4 | Rest | G |

Repeat the bar. Next, keep the same left-hand bass and swap the right-hand notes around. Notice that the two hands are not competing: the bass anchors the bar, and the upper notes provide movement.

## Step 3: Add four chord roots

Now play one bass note per bar over **C | G | Am | F**:

- Bar 1: left-hand **C**
- Bar 2: left-hand **G**
- Bar 3: left-hand **A**
- Bar 4: left-hand **F**

Repeat these four bars. With your right hand, improvise using **C, D, E, G and A**. These five notes form the C major pentatonic scale and give you a compact set of sounds to explore over the progression.

If changing the bass causes you to lose your place, stop the melody for a round and play only the roots. Then add the right hand again.

## Step 4: Let the hands play at different moments

Try playing the bass on beat 1 while the right hand rests. Play a right-hand note on beat 2, then another on beat 4. Leave beat 3 silent.

When that feels natural, move the second right-hand note to the **and** after beat 3. Count **1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and** so the off-beat has a clear place.

You are beginning to develop hand independence: one hand supplies a dependable pattern while the other varies the rhythm.

## Step 5: Turn the exercise into a musical conversation

Create a two-bar question followed by a two-bar answer. Keep the left-hand roots moving steadily. Make the answer begin with the same rhythm but finish on a different note.

To develop the sound further, try adding a fifth above each bass note on beat 3: C–G for the C bar, G–D for G, A–E for Am, and F–C for F. Only add this when the simple version is secure.

## If your hands keep stopping

- **Simplify the left hand.** Use one bass note every four beats.
- **Limit the right hand.** Two or three notes are enough for an interesting phrase.
- **Count aloud.** Silence and rests are part of the rhythm, not mistakes.
- **Practise separate hands briefly.** Then combine them for just one bar.
- **Avoid speeding up too early.** Smooth changes are more useful than fast ones.
