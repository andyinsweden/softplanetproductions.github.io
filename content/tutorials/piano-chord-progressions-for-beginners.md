You do not need dozens of chords to start improvising on piano. A **chord progression** is simply a sequence of harmonies that repeats or moves towards a musical ending. Once your hands know a short progression, you can spend more attention on listening and inventing melodies.

Here are four loops you can play in the key of C major, mostly using white keys. Start with one chord per bar in **4/4 time**: count **1, 2, 3, 4** slowly, then change chord on the next **1**.

## First, learn the four basic chords

Play these three-note chords with your right hand. For now, play one lower bass note with your left hand.

| Chord | Right-hand notes | Left-hand bass |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C major (C) | C–E–G | C |
| G major (G) | G–B–D | G |
| A minor (Am) | A–C–E | A |
| F major (F) | F–A–C | F |

You can hold each chord for four beats or play it gently on every beat. The second option is useful for building a steady pulse. If the hands feel awkward, practise the bass notes alone first.

## Four easy piano chord progressions

### 1. C–G–Am–F: the pop loop

Play **C | G | Am | F** and repeat. In Roman numerals this is **I–V–vi–IV** in C major. Many pop-style harmonies use this pattern or a variation of it.

Try a right-hand melody using the notes **C, D, E, G and A**. These are the notes of the **C major pentatonic scale**. Start with only two melody notes and add more when your timing feels steady.

### 2. C–F–G–C: leave home and return

Play **C | F | G | C** (**I–IV–V–I**). Listen for the sense of resolution when the G chord returns to C. Make the final C chord last for two bars to give the phrase a clear ending.

### 3. Am–F–C–G: a minor-coloured opening

Play **Am | F | C | G** (**vi–IV–I–V**). These chords belong to the same key, but starting on A minor can change the emotional character.

Use the same five melody notes. Compare a phrase beginning on A with one beginning on C, and notice how the harmony changes what you hear.

### 4. C–Am–F–G: a different journey through the same chords

Play **C | Am | F | G** (**I–vi–IV–V**). Hold the G chord for an extra bar before returning to C. This makes an ideal framework for a short question-and-answer melody.

## Make chord changes smoother with inversions

Jumping between root-position chords is not always comfortable. An **inversion** puts a different chord note at the bottom, making it easier to keep the hand in one area of the keyboard.

For the first loop, experiment with these right-hand shapes:

- **C:** C–E–G
- **G:** B–D–G
- **Am:** C–E–A
- **F:** C–F–A

Use the [piano chord finder](/tools/chord-finder/) to hear these inversions or identify a new shape from its notes. All four are still the same harmonies as before. Play each shape slowly and look for notes you can keep under the same finger. Add the left-hand root notes only when the chord changes feel easy.

## A ten-minute piano improvisation practice

1. **Two minutes:** Play left-hand bass notes for C–G–Am–F.
2. **Three minutes:** Add a right-hand chord on beat 1 of each bar.
3. **Three minutes:** Keep the bass steady and invent a short right-hand melody using C, D, E, G and A.
4. **Two minutes:** Repeat one two-bar phrase, changing only its ending.

Record a short attempt, even if it feels imperfect. On the next day, practise the part you found most difficult.
