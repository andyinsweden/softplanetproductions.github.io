You can play a sonata beautifully from the score, yet freeze when a friend asks you to play a familiar tune without music. The problem isn't a lack of musical ability: reading and playing by ear draw on different habits.

Classical training has already given you an enormous head start — touch, phrasing, coordination and a vocabulary of harmony. Now it's time to reconnect those skills with your listening.

## 1. Hear the distance between notes

Begin with a melody you can sing from memory. Find its first note, sing the next one and decide whether it moves up, down or stays the same. Then locate it on the keyboard.

Try C–E–G–A. The movements are a major third, a minor third and a major second. But don't only name the intervals: listen to their character.

**Exercise:** Sing a four-note phrase, play it, then move it to another starting note. This teaches relationships rather than fixed finger positions.

## 2. Recognise chord functions

In a major key, chords can be described as **I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi and vii°**. These numbers tell you how each chord relates to the home key.

Try **I–V–vi–IV**: C–G–Am–F in C major, or G–D–Em–C in G major. Notice the sense of departure and return. Don't expect every tune to follow this pattern — it's simply an excellent training loop.

Play each chord slowly and sing the bass note before pressing it.

## 3. Listen to bass movement

The lowest notes often reveal the harmonic direction, though bass lines can include passing notes and inversions rather than just chord roots.

With a short recording, tap the beat and identify where the harmony changes. Sing the lowest note you hear, then try it at the piano. Once you have a candidate bass line, test chords above it rather than assuming you've already found the answer.

## 4. Use inversions for smooth changes

Root-position triads can make accompaniment sound jumpy. Keep shared notes where possible.

For C–G–Am–F, try these right-hand voicings:

- **C:** G–C–E
- **G:** G–B–D
- **Am:** A–C–E
- **F:** A–C–F

Let the left hand provide the roots. Hear how the harmony changes while the right hand travels only a short distance? That's voice leading — a skill your classical repertoire has been teaching you all along.

## 5. Make mistakes useful

A note outside the chord doesn't automatically ruin a phrase. Hold it, listen, then try moving by a semitone or step towards a note that resolves comfortably. Sometimes the dissonance is the interesting part.

**Ten-minute challenge:** Spend three minutes finding a melody, three identifying its bass movement and four adding simple harmony. Make a short recording and compare it with tomorrow's attempt.

## From the score to your own ideas

Playing by ear won't replace score reading; it expands what you can do at the piano. Practise little and often, and resist judging an exploratory session by concert-performance standards.
