Three flutes, five keyboards, a trumpet, several enthusiastic singers — and no bass player. Sound familiar? Most school ensembles aren't neatly balanced orchestras. **The best school arrangement begins with the pupils in front of you**, not an imaginary perfect line-up.

Here is a flexible approach to turning that unpredictable collection of musicians into a group that enjoys rehearsing and sounds convincing on stage.

## 1. Create a musical anchor

Before writing twenty separate parts, establish the melody, chord symbols and a dependable piano or keyboard accompaniment. That reduced score becomes your safety net: if someone is absent on concert day, the music still works.

Try a basic eight-bar arrangement with melody in the right hand and chord roots in the left. Once the harmonic shape is clear, distribute material to other instruments.

## 2. Write for players, not just instruments

A beginner flute player might manage a repeated two-note figure beautifully, while a more experienced player can take a countermelody. Assign difficulty according to the musician, not the reputation of the instrument.

For each part, ask: Is the range comfortable? Is the rhythm readable? Does the student have enough time to breathe or turn a page? If a part can be simplified without losing the effect, simplify it.

**Practical example:** With five keyboards, give one the chord foundation, one a bass line, one the melody and two simple rhythmic or sustained textures. Everybody contributes without competing for the same register.

## 3. Add vocal harmony gradually

Start by making the melody secure and comfortable for the actual singers. Then introduce a second line, perhaps a sustained chord tone or a simple parallel movement at the end of phrases. Rehearse the two lines independently before combining them.

Avoid assuming a voice type from the way a pupil speaks. Check comfortable singing ranges through gentle warm-ups and adapt parts as voices develop.

## 4. Save time in Sibelius

Build repeating rhythmic patterns once, then reuse and edit them. Enter chord symbols early to keep the harmony visible. When working with clarinets, trumpets or saxophones, check the display between concert pitch and transposed score before distributing parts.

For more notation ideas, explore our [Sibelius Quick Tips](/tutorials/sibelius-quick-tips/) and [Sibelius Troubleshooting](/tutorials/sibelius-troubleshooting/) guides.

One essential final check: examine each printed part, not only the conductor's score. Missing rests, awkward page turns and unclear repeats become rehearsal problems quickly.

## 5. Treat the first rehearsal as part of arranging

Your first run-through is a test, not a verdict. Keep a pencil handy, note the moments that stall and ask players which passages feel uncomfortable. Adjust the arrangement and print cleaner parts before the next session.

A short confident performance is often more satisfying than an ambitious score that never quite comes together.

## A checklist before the concert

- Is the melody clear, even when a player is missing?
- Does each performer have a manageable, worthwhile role?
- Are transpositions, dynamics and repeats checked?
- Have you rehearsed transitions and endings?
- Do the individual parts print legibly?
