What happens when you sit at the piano without a score? For some players, the answer is silence. But **piano improvisation isn't a gift reserved for jazz virtuosos**. It's a skill you can develop by experimenting with a few musical ingredients, listening carefully and repeating short ideas.

The aim isn't to play a hundred notes. It's to make a handful of notes sound intentional.

## 1. Start with a progression you already know

Try four chords in C major: **C – G – Am – F**. Play one chord per bar in your left hand at a comfortable tempo. If block chords feel too heavy, begin with a single bass note for each chord: C, G, A, F.

Over the top, improvise a melody using **C, D, E, G and A**. These five notes create a friendly starting palette. Listen to what happens when you hold a note across a chord change: the same note may suddenly feel different.

**Five-minute exercise:** Play the progression four times. On the first repeat, use just two melody notes; on the second, add a third. On the last two, keep the same short motif and change only its ending.

## 2. Let rhythm do some of the work

A memorable musical phrase often depends more on rhythm than note count. Play C–D–E–G once as four even notes, then again with a pause after the first note. Same pitches, different musical character.

Try leaving an entire beat silent. The space gives your next phrase somewhere to go.

## 3. Build a conversation, not a scale run

Think of a two-bar musical question followed by a two-bar answer. The answer can borrow the rhythm of the question but finish on a steadier note such as C over the C chord. Repetition creates recognition; variation keeps listeners interested.

Record 30 seconds on your phone. Listen back and identify the phrase you like most. Play it again tomorrow rather than starting from scratch.

## 4. Use chord tones as landing places

You don't need to avoid every note outside the chord. But strong-beat notes often sound settled when they belong to the underlying harmony. Over C major, listen to C, E and G; over A minor, try A, C and E.

Treat a surprising note as an invitation to listen and respond — not a reason to stop.

## 5. Practise one small skill every day

Consistency beats an occasional exhausting session. Here's a manageable ten-minute routine:

1. **Two minutes:** Play C–G–Am–F steadily.
2. **Three minutes:** Invent short right-hand questions and answers.
3. **Three minutes:** Keep one motif while changing its rhythm.
4. **Two minutes:** Record a take and note one improvement for next time.

## What should you learn next?

Once you can improvise freely over four chords, explore inversions, richer harmonies, different accompaniment patterns and playing in new keys.
