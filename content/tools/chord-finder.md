## Piano chord chart: 65 chord types in every root

Explore 780 root-and-type combinations across the 12 chromatic pitches, or 1,105 labelled combinations when enharmonic spellings such as C♯ and D♭ are counted separately. Popular chords appear first. Slash chords and inversions provide additional voicings.

| Chord family | Examples in C | What to listen for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Major and minor triads | C, Cm | Bright major or darker minor thirds |
| Power, diminished and augmented | C5, Cdim, Caug | Open fifths, tension and altered fifths |
| Suspended chords | Csus2, Csus4, C7sus4 | The third is replaced by a second or fourth |
| Sixth chords | C6, Cm6, C6/9 | Added colour without a conventional seventh |
| Seventh chords | C7, Cmaj7, Cm7, Cm(maj7), Cm7♭5, Cdim7 | Dominant tension, major and minor colours |
| Added-note chords | Cadd9, Cmadd11, Cadd13, C7add13 | Extra melodic colour without requiring every intervening extension |
| Ninth chords | C9, Cmaj9, Cm9, C9sus4 | Extended harmony with a ninth |
| Eleventh chords | C11, Cm11, Cmaj11 | Rich extended harmony |
| Thirteenth chords | C13, Cm13, Cmaj13, C13sus4 | Full extended chord tones |
| Altered jazz chords | C7♭9, C7♯9, C7♯11, C7♭13, C13♭9♯11, C7alt | Chromatic tension and colour |

For example, **C7** contains C–E–G–B♭, **Cm9** contains C–E♭–G–B♭–D, and the full theoretical **C13** voicing contains C–E–G–B♭–D–F–A. In real jazz and pop arrangements, players often omit some extended chord tones to suit the music. The finder shows its defined voicing rather than every possible fingering.

## How to use the interactive piano chord library

1. Choose a root such as C, F♯ or B♭.
2. Select a chord symbol from the list; common chords appear first.
3. Read the highlighted keys and note names on the virtual piano.
4. Press **Play chord** to hear the voicing, or play the notes individually.
5. Select an inversion to explore a different bass note.


## What chord is this? Identify a chord by its notes

Choose **Identify a chord**, then tap the piano keys or type notes such as **C E G**. Tap a highlighted key again to remove it. For a specific voicing, enter notes with octaves such as **E3 G3 C4**, or MIDI note numbers such as **60 64 67**.

When you type notes without octaves, the finder puts them in ascending order as you type them. If you include octaves or enter MIDI numbers, the lowest pitch determines the bass regardless of the order you type.

| Notes from lowest to highest | Chord symbol | What you’re playing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C–E–G | C | C major in root position |
| E–G–C | C/E | C major in first inversion |
| C–E♭–G–B♭ | Cm7 | C minor seventh |
| G–B–D–F | G7 | G dominant seventh |

The finder recognises 65 chord types, from major and minor triads to suspended, added-note and altered chords. It shows alternate names when they fit the same notes. For example, **C–E–G–A** can be **C6** or **Am7/C**. The key and the chords around it can help you choose the right name.

## Look up piano chord notes and inversions

Choose **Find keys**, select a root and a chord from the library, or enter a chord symbol such as **Fmaj7**, **B♭m9** or **C/E**. The highlighted piano keys show the voicing. Use **Play chord** to hear the notes together or **Play notes in order** to hear them individually.

The note after a slash specifies the bass. In **C/E**, the chord is C major and E is the lowest note. Use the **Inversion** menu to see how the same chord fits under your hands with a different chord tone in the bass.

## Use a computer keyboard or USB MIDI piano

Under **Identify a chord**, open **Computer keyboard and MIDI** to see the controls. Enable **Computer keys** to use your typing keyboard. To use a USB MIDI keyboard, connect it, choose **Connect MIDI** in a browser that supports Web MIDI and play the notes together. On a phone or tablet, tap the piano on screen or type the notes.
